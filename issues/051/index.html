01   OpenAI’s research blog, Nov 22 · 524 HN points this morning · authored by chief scientist Jakub Pachocki

🚦 OpenAI’s chief scientist publishes the internal perplexity curve - and proposes an industry speed limit.

Jakub Pachocki discloses OpenAI’s log-perplexity trajectory: GPT-3.5 at ~0.99, GPT-4 at ~0.91, below 0.8 today, ~0.76 when the math breakthroughs started. He expects new capabilities at ~0.68-0.7, warns a pretraining avalanche could come “as soon as 2027,” and proposes common limits labs would not cross until monitoring catches up.
SCENE

On Saturday, OpenAI’s chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published “Perplexity and Intelligence,” a post that does two unusual things at once: it hands competitors a ruler for OpenAI’s own progress, and it asks every lab to agree on where to stop - temporarily - until the safety instrumentation improves. His words on the disclosure: “Publishing our internal perplexity measurements took some consideration; it gives competitors a clearer picture of where we stand, but that concern is outweighed by the urgency.”

THE THING

The disclosed curve, all on OpenAI’s current main evaluation dataset: GPT-3.5 sat at ~0.99 log-perplexity, fluent conversation. GPT-4 at ~0.91, productive chain-of-thought. Current models are below 0.8, and at ~0.76 the models “started producing groundbreaking results” on open math problems. He expects further transformative capabilities in the ~0.68-0.7 window, concedes “I don’t know what those capabilities will be,” and warns that a sudden avalanche of pretraining advances could come as soon as 2027. The proposal: shared transparency standards, joint evaluation norms, and common development limits measured in perplexity that labs would commit not to cross until monitoring - for example a successor to chain-of-thought monitoring, which he says is already degrading - catches up. His own listed caveats: perplexity may not be comparable across labs, it is detached from downstream tasks, it does not capture reasoning gains, and nobody knows its floor.

✂️ STEAL IT

Steal the pacing pattern, whatever you think of the policy. Pick one substrate metric your team can actually compute - loss, eval score, defect rate - write down the thresholds that would change your behavior, and pre-commit the gates before the capability arrives. That is the entire proposal, and it works for a two-person infra team as well as a frontier lab. What you cannot steal: a common scale. Without one, every lab grades its own homework, which is exactly what this post is trying to end.

Watch  VERDICT

Read the post itself - it is short, and a frontier lab disclosing its internal gauge is rare. Hold the framing loosely: these are OpenAI’s self-published internal measurements attached to a policy proposal on OpenAI’s terms, and no other lab has signed on. This fails you if you treat the 2027 avalanche line as a forecast rather than a warning, or if you need a metric you can compare across vendors today - he says himself you cannot yet.

02  Baez’s Azimuth blog, Nov 22 · the formalization repo and its progress map · 178 HN points for the map alone

🎼 The human Riemann proof gets its own verification race: first explainer, public formalization, live progress map.

John Baez published the first readable sketch of Masullo’s 940-page manuscript - flagging the problems people have already found. A community Lean formalization is underway on GitHub, tracked by a public progress map. And two independent explainers now reconstruct the Alpöge-Claude proof for humans.
SCENE

While the Lean-certified Alpöge-Claude proof owned the weekend, the parallel human claim - Alessandro Masullo’s 940-page “The Music of the Primes,” posted Friday by Ravi Vakil - is going through the old-school version of the same machine. Yesterday mathematician John Baez published the first accessible account of what the manuscript actually tries, labeled by him as very tentative.

THE THING

Baez’s sketch, in one line: Masullo works through the explicit formula - the exact identity linking primes to zeros of the zeta function, “the music of the primes” in which each zero contributes one oscillating wave. Baez lists problems people have already noted and points to the two checks now running in public: an AI-assisted review organized by Eric Naslund, and a community Lean formalization on GitHub whose progress is tracked by a live map - a checklist of which of the 940 pages are machine-verified so far, which drew 178 HN points on its own. Status is unchanged from yesterday’s issue: a rebuttal manuscript appeared Friday, and Vakil’s bar stands - every reported issue must be resolved before the proof is accepted. Meanwhile, for the certified proof, two human-readable explanations appeared: Michael Rubinstein’s walkthrough of the zeta case and Jimmie Adriazola’s end-to-end reconstruction. The machine-checked proof is getting its human interface too.

✂️ STEAL IT

Steal the progress map. A 940-page verification effort would normally live in somebody’s inbox and a hundred “any updates?” threads. Instead it is a public artifact: every section a box, every box either checked or not, anyone free to pick an open one. That pattern works for any long review - a migration, an audit, a docs overhaul. Make the checklist the website.

Watch  VERDICT

Read Baez first: it is the only account of the human proof that a non-specialist can finish, and it is honest about its own tentativeness. Then glance at the progress map. This fails you if you read “formalization underway” as “proof correct” - the map is a checklist, not a verdict - or if you need the Alpöge-Claude proof explained at textbook depth today; the two reconstructions are still drafts.
