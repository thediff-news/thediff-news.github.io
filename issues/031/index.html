🧠 GPT-6 Astra fixes another model’s math by editing 0.016% of its weights

MIT CSAIL researchers show a frontier LLM reading raw neural-network weights to say what a network does, writing game-playing networks from scratch, and repairing Qwen3.5-0.8B’s reasoning loops with no training - MATH-500 jumps from 7.9% to 48.1%. Their numbers.
SCENE

Hand a human 17,253 raw neural-network weights and ask what the network was trained to do: nothing. Hand them to GPT-6 Astra and it answers "Breakout," 99% confident, with no tools. That is the party trick opening a paper from four MIT CSAIL researchers this week. The trick scales: asked to make Qwen3.5-0.8B better at math without any gradient training, Astra identified the weight issue behind the reasoning loops Qwen’s own model card warns about and edited 0.016% of the weights. MATH-500 went from 7.9% to 48.1%, instruction following tripled - and the authors say it generalizes out of the box.

THE THING

The authors - Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab - call it weight-space programming: a general-purpose LLM reading and writing a network’s weights directly around a natural-language spec, sometimes with no environment feedback at all. In their demos Astra writes small game-playing networks from scratch that outplay policies PPO trained for 100 million steps, and patches a stuck driving policy by editing 3.5% of its weights, from 0% to 100% success. Their framing: for decades we encoded our inductive biases into tiny symbolic optimizers like Adam; weights may soon be natively optimized by LLMs that deliberately reason about them.

✂️ STEAL IT

Steal the mental model: weights are becoming a source language, and reading what the optimizer learned may soon be as normal as reading a commit. Anything you can serialize, an agent can now plausibly inspect.

Watch  VERDICT

Watch for independent replication - every number here is the authors’ own, on small networks, from one frontier model. It fails you as a technique you can use today: nothing here is a tool you can point at your own checkpoints, and 0.8B-parameter fixes are a long way from production models.

🚨 A breach notice says the attacker was an autonomous AI hacking framework

WorkOS tells customers an intruder likely used ARTEX, an AI-assisted autonomous penetration-testing framework, to reach an internal sales API and pull CRM and support data out of Salesforce and Snowflake. Production systems and application data were not touched, it says.
SCENE

The first copy of this breach notice appeared where breach notices now appear: a customer pasting it to Hacker News before the vendor had published anything. WorkOS - the login plumbing behind a long list of SaaS - had detected anomalous traffic to an internal business system on October 30 at 16:39 UTC. The customer’s notification went further than most: the attack "was likely driven by ARTEX, an AI-assisted, autonomous penetration testing framework, from IP addresses based in Hong Kong and China."

THE THING

The official bulletin, published November 2, confirms the shape while pointedly not naming the framework: "Our investigation identified the use of an AI-assisted offensive security framework to automate reconnaissance and exploitation attempts." The intruder reached an internal API behind WorkOS’s sales and marketing tools and pulled data from Salesforce and Snowflake: business contacts, account and commercial information, aggregated de-identified usage metrics and support tickets - names, emails, phone numbers, company addresses. WorkOS says there is no evidence application data or production services were accessed, and that the incident was localized to CRM and customer-support data.

✂️ STEAL IT

Steal the threat-model update: "AI-assisted offensive security framework" is now a sentence in a real vendor’s breach bulletin, and the target was the soft perimeter - an internal API behind sales tools, not the production core. Your CRM integrations are attack surface with a login page.

Watch  VERDICT

Watch whether the ARTEX attribution gets independently confirmed - today it is WorkOS’s assessment inside a customer notice, and the public bulletin avoids the name. It fails you as a severity signal: by the company’s own account this is business-contact data, not keys, tokens or production access.

🏛️ Anthropic’s own filing: internal Claude use, at retail prices, passed employee pay

Mined from the IPO paperwork Anthropic filed in No. 024’s story: in April the value of Claude used inside Anthropic - priced as if it were a customer - exceeded monthly employee compensation excluding stock. By September usage was up 250-fold since January, and over 90% of new code was written by Claude.
SCENE

Every S-1 has a number that tells you how the company actually runs. In Anthropic’s, reporter Alistair Barr found it: starting in April, the value of the Claude AI Anthropic used internally - calculated at the retail prices it charges customers - exceeded what it paid employees each month, stock awards excluded. By September, that internal usage had grown more than 250-fold since January.

THE THING

The same reporting surfaces the companion stats: more than 90% of the code added to Anthropic’s software in September was written by Claude, and between December 2024 and September 2026 revenue grew roughly 86-fold while headcount grew about five-fold. Barr’s own caveat does real work: this is not cash out the door - Anthropic is valuing its own compute at list price, a bookkeeping choice that flatters the comparison. Read it as a statement about where the company thinks value sits, not a cost crisis.

✂️ STEAL IT

Steal the metric: tokens-per-employee is becoming a compensation-adjacent line item - Barr calls AI tokens the fourth component of pay alongside salary, bonus and equity. If you cannot say what your team’s AI usage would cost at retail, you do not know your real burn multiple.

Watch  VERDICT

Watch for more S-1 mining - the filing is fresh and every desk is pulling numbers from it this week. It fails you as audited fact: these are figures from Anthropic’s own filing as relayed by one outlet, and the retail-pricing caveat is load-bearing in the headline.

🗄️ OpenAI rebuilt Git’s backend because the agents pushed too much

Codex multiplied concurrent branches, pushes and CI fetches until monorepo reliability fell under 90%. The answer is Pando, a Git-compatible backend with horizontally scalable writes - median push latency cut nearly 5x, serving almost a petabyte of Git data a day.
SCENE

Somewhere inside OpenAI last year, Git started losing. Not Git the format - Git the server. Codex let every engineer run several changes in parallel, each push fanning out into CI jobs fetching the same commit, and the monorepo’s infrastructure started buckling: slow fetches, failed merges, reliability at times below 90%.

THE THING

The fix, described by Aaron Friel, Taylor Blau and Ted Nyman, is Pando: a new backend for Git repositories that keeps the Git protocol and client exactly as they are while rebuilding what sits behind them - horizontally scalable writes for concurrent pushes to many branches, and read throughput meant to saturate the client’s network. It now powers all development in OpenAI’s monorepo: median push latency down nearly 5x, nearly a petabyte of Git data served per day, and no workflow change for engineers or agents. The team says it will keep contributing to Git upstream "to support agent-scale work."

✂️ STEAL IT

Steal the capacity signal: agentic development multiplies version-control traffic per human - branches, pushes and CI fetches scale with agents, not headcount. Your Git host’s limits are now part of your agent budget.

Watch  VERDICT

Watch whether Pando or anything like it escapes the building - today it is internal infrastructure, not a product. It fails you as actionable tooling: you cannot adopt it, only heed it - if your monorepo already creaks under CI fan-out, agents will make it worse.

🤫 Glasses that hear the words you only mouth

Subvocal, a two-person YC F26 team, is building smart glasses that decode silent speech - mouthing with your lips closed - into text at the speed of talking, using radio-frequency sensing instead of electrodes.
SCENE

Two Georgia Tech CS students, Aarush and Arnav, were power users of AI dictation tools who kept hitting the same wall: you cannot talk to your computer in a library, an office or a bus. At Stanford’s TreeHacks in February they tried to recreate MIT’s AlterEgo silent-speech system in 36 hours with EMG electrodes stuck to a face. It sort of worked, letter by letter. Now it is a YC company with a very different sensor.

THE THING

The current Subvocal glasses use radio-frequency sensing - "the RF can see inside your mouth," the founders say - so the system decodes internal speech: mouthing words with your lips closed, no visible movement needed. Their demo has YC’s own Dave trying a pair, with text arriving at the speed of speech. The pitch is an always-available, socially invisible input channel for the AI tools you already talk to.

✂️ STEAL IT

Steal the interface insight: voice input’s real constraint was never accuracy - it is the social cost of being heard. Removing the sound, not improving the model, is what unlocks dictation in shared spaces.

Watch  VERDICT

Watch the independent demos - everything public so far is the founders’ own footage, and silent-speech promises have a long history of demo-to-product gaps. It fails you as something to buy: an early hardware startup showing prototypes, not a shipping device.

🧪 ONE THING TO TRY TONIGHT
Read a neural network’s mind at hobby scale.
Free and one evening: the weight-space paper’s opening trick - show a model raw weights, ask what the network does - needs no special access. Train or download a tiny network (a few thousand weights on MNIST or a toy game), dump the weights to text, and ask your favorite frontier model what the network was trained to do. Then ask it to make the network better, and diff what it changes. You will hit the limits fast - hitting them yourself is the point.
