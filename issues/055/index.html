01  workspaceupdates.googleblog.com, Nov 24 · 289 HN points

🤖 Google to AI agents: from February 2027 you reach Gmail, Drive and Calendar through Google’s MCP servers - or not at all.

The Workspace AI Tools Addendum takes effect February 1, 2027: any tool “that is or is driven by an AI Agent” must go through a Google MCP server or the Workspace CLI, stored embeddings and memory indexes are capped at 30 days, and Workspace data may never be used for training.
SCENE

Buried in the middle of a pleasant-sounding blog post - “Simplifying agent access while keeping organizations in control” - Google made eight Workspace MCP servers generally available on November 24, covering Gmail, Drive, Docs, Sheets, Slides, Calendar, Chat and Tasks, plus a Workspace CLI for terminal-based agents. Then it attached the terms. The new Workspace AI Tools Addendum is the part every agent builder has to read, and the discussion on Hacker News did.

THE THING

The addendum’s clause 1: “The GWS Access Tool must use a Google MCP where the GWS Access Tool is or is driven by an AI Agent, an AI Harness or an AI model or service employing an AI model,” and you may not obfuscate a tool to avoid that classification. Google’s quota page dates it: the addendum takes effect February 1, 2027, with MCP requirement enforcement the same day, and billing enforcement April 1, 2027. The data rules bite harder than the routing: no storing “Google Technical Information” - defined to include vector embeddings, memory indexes and user profiles - longer than 30 days; delete everything when the user disconnects; no processing beyond the active, user-authorized task; no training, fine-tuning, eval datasets or distillation; no building a competing service. Violation lets Google restrict or suspend access immediately.

✂️ STEAL IT

If your product reads a user’s Gmail, your 2027 architecture just got decided for you: plan the MCP migration now, and audit anything you cache - a vector index of someone’s inbox older than 30 days becomes a terms violation, so design retention as a feature, not an afterthought. Note the carve-out Google is selling: MCP tools return “synthesized summaries within Workspace’s boundary,” which Google pitches as token-efficient and which also means the raw data stops leaving the building. Non-AI integrations are untouched - the trigger is an agent in the loop.

Watch  VERDICT

Fourteen months out, with the tooling already GA: build against the MCP servers now and the deadline is a non-event; keep shipping direct-REST agent integrations and February 2027 is a migration you do under pressure. The failure mode is treating the 30-day data cap as a detail - for memory-first agent products it is the whole design.

02  janlukas.blog, Nov 26 · 443 HN points

🏠 “AI made self-hosting fun again” - the home-lab revival essay that beat the Google lockdown to the top of HN.

A developer explains how coding agents removed the friction that made self-hosting a second job: infrastructure as Git-managed Docker Compose, a NixOS home server, and his own Debian bootc image on the way. His story, his setup.
SCENE

Jan-Lukas Else self-hosted for years, hit the wall everyone hits - every service a snowflake, every upgrade a weekend - and walked away from most of it. What brought him back was not a new orchestrator. It was an AI coding agent that answers the annoying questions: what goes in this Compose file, why does this container fail health checks, how do I do this in Nix. His essay spent Thanksgiving morning at the top of Hacker News.

THE THING

The shape of his setup: every service is a Docker Compose project in Git, so the infrastructure is diffable, reviewable and restorable; the home server now runs NixOS, where he says the agent shines because the system is declarative enough for a model to reason about; and he is building his own Debian bootc image for his VPS, treating the OS as a container artifact. The pattern underneath the essay: agents did not make self-hosting possible, they made the learning curve boring, and boring is what makes a hobby stick.

✂️ STEAL IT

Steal the workflow, not the stack: infra in Git first, so every agent suggestion becomes a reviewable pull request instead of a mystery state on a box. The corollary he does not dwell on: an agent writing your Compose files is also an agent choosing your exposed ports, so the diff review is the security boundary, not a formality.

Watch  VERDICT

A person doing a thing, and a signal: the same agents that write code are quietly reviving self-hosting as a mainstream hobby, which pulls demand toward homelab hardware, NixOS and bootc-style OS images. The failure mode is letting the agent own the config - if you cannot read what it wrote, you run infrastructure you do not have.

ALSO ON THE RADAR

An AMD-only llama.cpp fork claims 55 tok/s - and a warning about bespoke engines that change what your model computes. devnull.land recommends llama-cpp-rdna-boosts, a patchset re-applied onto a pinned llama.cpp, over upstream for AMD GPUs: his token generation went 35 to 55 tok/s and prefill 700 to 1,300 tok/s - his hardware, his measurements (310 HN points). The interesting half is his warning: a wave of vibe-coded bespoke engines (Strata, Basalt, RustingStrata) now chase speed by changing the computation itself - Strata, he writes, polls VRAM-resident experts instead of the correctly routed ones, so the benchmark wins and the model quietly gets dumber. His tests, his framing; the general point stands: a faster engine is a different model until proven otherwise. devnull.land: the fork and the warning →

First outside check of the ω = 9/4 proof: the Lean compiles - and claims more than the paper proves. Sysnote rebuilt the Lean development from OpenAI’s November 24 math drop (covered in No. 054): the proof compiles, but the formal statement asserts more about rectangular matrices than the paper proves, and omits the paper’s bound for each fixed n. This is exactly what the verification era is supposed to surface: not “the proof is wrong” - nobody is saying that - but a gap between what a lab claims and what its certificate certifies. One independent analysis; OpenAI has not responded at press time. sysnote: the check →

Moat pitches “the Costco of AI” - $5 a month for wholesale tokens, no hourly caps. Command Code’s Moat (263 HN points): a $5/month membership buys access to frontier-ish open models - GLM 5.3, DeepSeek v4.1, Kimi K3 and friends - at claimed wholesale per-token prices with no 5-hour or weekly limits, pooled buying power doing the discounting. The comparison page targets Claude-style subscription limits directly: $100/month and capped versus $5 and uncapped, their framing. The questions the page does not answer - which providers serve the weights, what happens to prompts, who eats the loss when wholesale math fails - are the ones to ask before routing work traffic through it. 🧪 ONE THING TO TRY TONIGHT
Five minutes: boot three Linux VMs side by side in one browser tab.
Side by Side (michidk.github.io/sidebyside, 124 HN points) opens one URL in Firefox, Chromium and Epiphany at once - three real browsers, each in its own Linux VM inside your tab, no server behind the page. Type a URL, wait the 1-3 minutes the first boot takes (later visits reuse the cache), and compare Gecko, Blink and WebKit rendering your project for real. You can add Firefox ESR and NetSurf, and flip every pane to a phone-sized window. You need 8 GB of RAM. The failure mode is trying it on a laptop that is already swapping.
